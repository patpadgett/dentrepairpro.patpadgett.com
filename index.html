What PDR fixes, and what it doesn't.

Paintless dent repair has one hard rule: the paint has to be sound. Inside that rule it covers more than most people expect. Outside it, a body shop is the honest answer, and you should hear that on the phone, not after a drive.

Good candidates

  • Door dings and parking-lot dents
  • Hail, from a few hits to a whole roof
  • Dents up to roughly baseball size on flat or gently curved panels
  • Soft creases without sharp folds
  • Dents on doors, fenders, hoods, roofs, and quarter panels
  • Lease-return and resale touch-ups

Depends on the inspection

  • Dents on a body line, crease, or panel edge
  • Deep or sharp dents where the metal may be stretched
  • Dents over inner bracing with no access behind them
  • Aluminum panels, which hold less memory than steel
  • Panels that have been filled or repainted before
  • Light scratches in the paint over the dent

Not a PDR job

  • Cracked, chipped, or flaking paint
  • Metal torn or stretched by a hard impact
  • Plastic bumper covers
  • Collision damage that moved the panel off its mounts
  • Rust under the dent

Not sure which column you're in? That is what the call is for. Read your dent first and the answer comes faster.

How a dent comes out.

No sanding, no filler, no primer, no paint. The technician works the metal itself, reading every push in the reflection of the board.

Why not just the body shop?

Body shops are the right call for cracked paint and crumpled metal. For a dent in sound paint, PDR usually gets to the same place with less time, less cost, and the original paint still on the car. Here is what changes.

Questions people ask on the phone.

Short answers here. The long ones are a phone call away.

Will the repair show?

On a typical door ding in sound paint, no: nothing is sanded, filled, or sprayed, so there is nothing to mismatch and nothing to blend. The board is the test: when the reflected lines run straight across the spot, the panel is flat. Very deep or stretched dents can leave faint texture, and you should hear that from John before the work starts, not after.

Does PDR work on hail?

Hail is where PDR earned its reputation. Dozens of shallow dents in sound paint are exactly what the method is for, and the alternative is repainting a roof, a hood, and a trunk lid. Many comprehensive insurance policies cover hail repair; check yours before you call so you know which conversation you are having.

What does it cost?

PDR is generally priced by the dent: size, depth, where it sits on the panel, and how hard it is to reach behind. The number comes from looking at the dent under the board, so a phone call sets expectations and the inspection sets the price. There is no filler or paint in the job, which is the main reason it usually costs less than bodywork.

How long does it take?

A single door ding is often an hour or two of work. Several dents, or a dent that needs trim removed for access, take longer. Hail is its own conversation and depends on the count. Either way there is nothing to dry or cure, so the car is ready as soon as the work is finished.

Is it really paintless?

Yes. No sanding, no filler, no primer, no paint. That is also the limit of the method: if the paint over the dent is already cracked or chipped, pushing the metal back will not fix the paint, and the honest answer is a body shop.

My panel is aluminum. Does that matter?

It does. Aluminum holds less memory than steel and work-hardens as it is pushed, so it asks for more skill, more time, and sometimes heat. Many hoods, tailgates, and whole truck bodies are aluminum now. Mention it when you call.
